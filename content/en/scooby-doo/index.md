# Resonance: Scooby-Doo

**Resonance: Scooby-Doo** is a lightweight Resonance implementation designed as a compression test for the framework.

Where **Glorantha Perspectives** explores how deeply Resonance can descend into a world, Scooby-Doo explores the opposite extreme: how many rules, how much lore, and how much preparation can be removed while still preserving a strongly characterized experience?

This document contains the complete playable implementation: character creation, Prisms, GM advice, and a ready-to-play scenario.

It assumes familiarity with the general principles of [Resonance](https://aleascript.github.io/resonance/en/): Intention, Focus, Zoom, Bets, relevance, and fictional interpretation.

> **The goal is not to simulate Scooby-Doo.**  
> **The goal is to make effective play naturally push the table toward producing Scooby-Doo.**

---

# 1. The world in a few lines

You are a group of college friends on summer vacation.

You have fixed up an old van and are driving across the United States, listening to music, eating whatever you can find, and stopping at places where no reasonable person would ever stop.

Inevitably, you run into:

- an abandoned motel;
- a closed amusement park;
- a haunted mine;
- an empty museum;
- an overwhelmed sheriff;
- a local notable who is far too helpful;
- a fluorescent monster that looks supernatural but is probably wearing a rubber mask.

The tone is that of a cartoon mystery.

Fear exists, but it is funny.

Danger exists, but it is not trying to kill the characters.

Coincidences are acceptable when they are funny and coherent with the trope.

Complicated plans almost always go wrong.

And yet, somehow, the group solves the mystery.

# 2. Creating the characters

Each player chooses an archetype.

The lists below are not closed sets of abilities. They are **fictional truths** meant to make the character's relevance space immediately visible.

Keep the keywords you enjoy, cross out the ones you do not, and add a few of your own.

The character is ready as soon as the table understands who they are.

## The Handsome Guy with the terrible plan

*The self-appointed leader. Charismatic, confident, and always ready to solve a simple problem with a Rube Goldberg machine.*

### Possible keywords

- square jaw;
- ultra-bright smile;
- supremely confident;
- natural authority;
- former scout leader;
- IQ inversely proportional to good looks;
- obsessed with elaborate traps;
- "Let's split up";
- driver's license;
- absolute faith in his own plan.

### Possible relationships

- secretly in love with the Pretty Airhead;
- convinced the Brain admires his plans;
- considers the Clumsy Duo a permanent logistical hazard;
- looks at himself a little too often in the Van's rear-view mirror.

## The Pretty Airhead

*The one who attracts trouble like a magnet and regularly discovers essential things without understanding how.*

### Possible keywords

- always impeccable wardrobe;
- refreshing naivety;
- piercing scream;
- danger-prone;
- improbable handbag;
- tactical makeup;
- black belt in shopping;
- hairpin lockpicking;
- mysterious ability to be in exactly the wrong place.

### Possible relationships

- flirts with the monster without realizing it;
- treats the Brain as her best friend even when she is not listening to her;
- thinks the Handsome Guy's plans are "super smart";
- always has something unexpected for the Clumsy Duo.

## The Brain

*The brains of the group. Skeptical, methodical, and probably the only person who thinks a clue is more interesting than a sandwich.*

### Possible keywords

- severe myopia;
- "My glasses!";
- turtleneck sweater;
- absolute skepticism;
- sarcasm;
- walking encyclopedia;
- relentless deduction;
- incomprehensible scientific jargon;
- crawls on the floor to inspect a trace;
- always finds the hidden switch.

### Possible relationships

- secretly in love with the Handsome Guy;
- despairing at the group's intellectual level;
- reluctantly protects the Clumsy Duo;
- the only person who approximately knows how to repair the Van.

## The Clumsy Duo — two players

*The magnificent cowards. They mostly think about eating and running away. That is often what saves everyone.*

The Duo is played by **two distinct players**.

### The Space Cadet

Possible keywords:

- always spaced out;
- absurd metabolism;
- Olympic-level coward;
- "No way!";
- stomach on legs;
- appetite of destruction;
- surprisingly fast legs when something growls behind him.

### The Animal Companion

It can be a talking dog, but also a raccoon, giant iguana, or any other creature the table is willing to live with for two hours.

Possible keywords:

- human-like expressions;
- championship coward;
- easily bribed with a treat;
- nose for trouble;
- able to disguise itself as a waiter in three seconds;
- approximate understanding of human speech.

### Shared relationship

They are inseparable.

They have their own language, share food, hide together, and paradoxically become more effective when they panic side by side.

That relationship is written directly into their Prism.

# 3. The fifth member: the Van

The Van has a small shared sheet created by the whole table.

Choose or invent a few truths in each family.

## Its appearance

- psychedelic paint job;
- rust hidden under stickers;
- right headlight that blinks by itself;
- dubious curtains;
- ridiculous name painted on the side.

## How it runs

- engine that coughs whenever everyone panics;
- breaks down in the middle of nowhere;
- starts instantly, but only when nobody is in a hurry;
- alarming handbrake;
- radio that receives a single station.

## Its interior

- secret snack reserve;
- questionable mattress;
- wildly oversized sound system;
- the Brain's improvised laboratory;
- useless equipment that will probably become essential at the end.

The Van is not mere decoration.

Its truths can become Bets, cause problems, provide solutions, or determine why the group happens to stop directly in front of the haunted motel the scenario needed.

# 4. The grammar of relevance

Scooby-Doo does not require an encyclopedia of lore.

Its identity comes primarily from what the game teaches the table to notice.

The following truths naturally tend to become relevant:

- fear;
- hunger;
- snacks;
- clues;
- disguises;
- secret passages;
- overcomplicated plans;
- splitting up the group;
- absurd coincidences;
- defective machines;
- relationships among the team members;
- personal gimmicks;
- signs of a hoax;
- the economic motive behind the "supernatural";
- the mask behind the monster.

This list does not forbid anything else.

It simply indicates **where this game likes to look**.

# 5. Common rules

Resonance: Scooby-Doo keeps the general Resonance loop.

1. The player states their **Intention**.
2. The table clarifies the **Focus** and its **Zoom** if necessary.
3. Fictional truths that genuinely matter become **Bets**.
4. Each Bet normally contributes **one D6**.
5. The character resolves according to their **Prism**.
6. The result is interpreted from the scene and the Bets.

## The rule everyone can remember

> **An even result is a Success.**  
> **An odd result is normally not a Success.**

This rule gives the game a common language even when some Prisms add patterns, shift interpretation, or produce a standalone result.

## The table does not hunt for bonuses

Keywords on the sheet are reminders of fiction, not buttons to activate.

The GM can pitch the game like this:

> "Tell me what you do and what genuinely matters in the scene. Every relevant truth becomes a Bet. Even numbers are Successes. But the world does not answer each of you in the same way. Play your characters to the hilt; the Prisms will do the rest."

## Tone contract

In this implementation:

- characters do not die because of a bad roll;
- the monster tries to frighten, capture, lock up, separate, or ridicule them;
- failure should create movement rather than stop the investigation;
- consequences can be absurd without becoming arbitrary;
- the GM adjudicates relevance quickly in order to preserve the pace.

# 6. The Prisms

Each archetype has its own way of transforming uncertainty.

These Prisms are deliberately asymmetric.

The goal is not for them to be identical in abstract power, but for each one to push its player toward a different, recognizable behavior.

## The Clumsy Duo — Cross-Resonance

The Duo does not necessarily play against an opposing pool.

Each player gathers **their own pool of Bets** and rolls separately.

### Normal Successes

Every even result rolled by either member of the Duo normally produces one Success.

Then add the Successes from both pools together.

### Cross-pairs of odd results

Their clumsiness becomes a resource when it resonates between them.

For each odd value `1`, `3`, or `5`, compare the two pools.

Each pair made of one die from each character showing **the same odd value** produces **one additional Success**.

A die can belong to only one cross-pair.

In other words, for each odd value, the number of additional Successes equals the smaller number of occurrences of that value across the two pools.

Example:

- Shaggy rolls `3, 3, 4, 5`;
- Scooby rolls `3, 2, 5, 5`.

The even results already produce two Successes: `4` and `2`.

The pools also contain:

- one cross-paired `3` → +1 Success;
- one cross-paired `5` → +1 Success.

Total: **4 Successes**.

Shaggy's two `3`s therefore do not create a Success with each other. Resonance has to cross between the two characters.

> **They are mechanically better when they panic together.**

### Reading the result

The number of Successes primarily measures **the scale of the useful chaos** their action produces.

A small success may simply let them escape.

A large success may send them racing across a kitchen on a cart, knocking over a shelf, revealing a secret passage, dropping the monster into its own trap, and finishing the chase with a sandwich still intact.

The result can stand on its own: it does not need an opposing pool to make sense.

## The Brain — Logic

The Brain uses the form of resolution closest to the standard configuration.

She gathers her Bets.

The GM gathers the Bets of adversity or the obstacle.

Both pools are rolled: even numbers are Successes and the totals are compared.

Her Prism is deliberately restrained.

She lives in a world where:

- a clue must have a cause;
- a hypothesis must survive contact with the facts;
- a secret door probably has a mechanism;
- a fluorescent ghost almost certainly bought its paint somewhere.

A Victory produces a rational discovery.

A Defeat should not block the investigation: it may make her lose her glasses, follow a false lead, get covered in soot, or obtain the clue with a complication.

> **She is the person trying to play a rational detective game in the middle of a cartoon.**

## The Handsome Guy — Misalignment

The Handsome Guy generally makes a classic opposed roll: evens, Successes, comparison.

But his result resonates **sideways**.

### If he wins

His plan works exactly as intended... but on the wrong target, at the wrong time, or in a way that creates a new problem.

The net catches the Clumsy Duo.

The cage drops perfectly, but the monster was already behind him.

The door locks exactly as planned, with the entire team on the wrong side.

### If he loses

His plan collapses spectacularly... and the disaster accidentally produces the effect the group actually needed.

The pulley snaps and knocks out the monster.

The giant fan tears away the scenery and reveals the secret passage.

The trap triggers too early, scares the ghost away, and tears off a piece of its mask.

### Interpretation

The roll therefore does not merely answer "does the plan work?"

It answers:

> **How does the world transform the Handsome Guy's confidence in his own plan?**

This Prism is deliberately the most experimental in the implementation. The Handsome Guy's tendency to **split up the group** is still carried primarily by his keywords and the grammar of relevance rather than by a mechanical incentive as strong as the Clumsy Duo's.

## The Pretty Airhead — Meaningful Accident

The Pretty Airhead can use dice of a distinct color if the table has some. This is not mandatory, but it makes her patterns immediately visible.

She can count evens as normal Successes when an opposition requires it.

Her Prism mainly adds two ways to produce a particular event.

### Accidental Charm

When the monster has an opposing pool, look at its highest result.

If at least one of the Pretty Airhead's dice shows exactly the same value, she produces a moment of **Accidental Charm**.

The monster becomes distracted, unsettled, touched, or temporarily unable to continue its action normally.

She may drop her mirror; the monster picks it up.

She may recognize a familiar detail under the mask and create an awkward silence.

She may simply speak to it as if it were a perfectly ordinary person at the exact moment everyone else is screaming.

This pattern does not necessarily replace the main result. It adds a fictional event the GM must incorporate.

### The story-changing "Oops"

The Pretty Airhead looks for **pretty patterns**.

If her roll contains a sequence of at least three consecutive values — for example `2-3-4` or `3-4-5` — or at least three identical dice, she triggers an **Oops**.

The Oops shifts the scene in a significant and accidental way.

She leans against a bookshelf to redo her shoelace and opens the secret passage.

She presses the red button while looking for the light switch and disables the monster's mechanism.

She trips on a carpet and reveals the trapdoor the Brain has been looking for for ten minutes.

The Oops can occur whether or not the main roll is victorious.

### What if nothing special appears?

She simply suffers the normal direction of the roll.

Within the game's tone, however, a Defeat never means serious violence or death.

She may:

- break a nail;
- be separated from the group;
- lose her bag;
- fall through a trapdoor;
- get locked in a VIP room with a tray of canapés;
- draw the monster somewhere else.

# 7. Advice for the GM

## Keep the investigation moving

A failure should not make the mystery unsolvable.

It changes how information is obtained, what it costs, or the situation in which the group discovers it.

The Brain may fail while examining a boiler, lose her glasses, and land directly on the dry-cleaning ticket that gives away the culprit.

The Duo may run in the wrong direction and accidentally burst into the room containing the phosphorescent paint.

## Adjudicate Bets quickly

The main pacing risk is turning the relevance conversation into a negotiation over bonuses.

In a two-hour game, the right rhythm is:

> **"Yes, that matters."**  
> **"No, not for this Focus."**  
> **"That is already contained in this other Bet."**  
> **"Excellent. Take the die."**

Favor proposals that:

- are immediately readable in the fiction;
- reinforce the trope;
- make the table laugh;
- create new possibilities for interpretation.

## Make keywords resonate against the characters

A truth is not always favorable.

*Severe myopia* can explain why the Brain misses something.

*Temperamental engine* can make the Van stop in front of the motel.

*Obsessed with elaborate traps* can be exactly why the Handsome Guy's plan requires fifteen minutes of preparation and six absurd objects.

## Cut when the trope has done its job

A chase is funny as long as it escalates.

An investigation is interesting as long as the players discover something.

A trap is entertaining as long as it still threatens to work.

Once the scene has produced its moment, move on.

# 8. Scenario: The Mystery of the Last Fry Motel

This scenario is designed for a game of roughly **two hours**, including character creation.

## The pitch

It is night.

Rain is pouring down.

The Van breaks down on an abandoned road. Immediately use one of its keywords: black smoke, temperamental engine, questionable fuel gauge, or any other truth chosen by the table.

The only lit building for miles is the **Bates Burger & Motel**, a 1950s roadside stop that seems to have missed several decades of modernization.

The elderly manager, **Mr. Grossi**, greets the group while trembling:

> "Run... The Ghost of the Mad Cook is back! It haunts the kitchen with its fluorescent spatula!"

Naturally, nobody actually runs away.

## The monster

### The Ghost of the Mad Cook

A specter covered in glistening grease, wearing a shredded chef's hat and wielding a phosphorescent spatula.

It screams terrifying recipes.

It appears to walk through walls.

It appears on one floor and immediately vanishes to another.

It smells faintly of fresh paint.

## The truth

The Ghost is actually **Mr. Zellani**, a local real-estate developer.

Beneath the motel is an old Prohibition-era smuggling cellar containing a collection of rare liquor worth a fortune.

Zellani wants to frighten Mr. Grossi into selling him the motel for almost nothing.

His appearances rely on:

- a costume;
- phosphorescent paint;
- a network of dumbwaiters and service passages;
- a few sound effects;
- the certainty that no sensible person will ever come investigate what is happening here.

He did not plan for the Van.

## The three Keys

To solve the mystery, the group must uncover three families of information.

Players do not need to discover them in a particular order or at the places suggested below.

### Key 1 — the Motive

Someone wants Grossi gone because the land or building has hidden value.

Possible clues:

- aggressive purchase offers signed by Zellani;
- an old plan showing a sealed cellar;
- old bottles discovered behind a wall;
- a delivery ledger from Prohibition;
- Grossi suddenly remembering that his grandfather used to talk about "the basement we were never supposed to open."

### Key 2 — the Trick

The ghost uses a physical device.

Possible clues:

- phosphorescent paint;
- traces of machine grease;
- a plan of the dumbwaiter network;
- nylon line;
- hidden speaker;
- a piece of costume caught on a trapdoor.

### Key 3 — the Culprit

The clues must ultimately point to Zellani.

Possible clues:

- dry-cleaning ticket;
- paint invoice;
- costume button;
- shoeprint;
- real-estate contract;
- recognizable recorded voice;
- absurdly obvious monogram.

Once the three Keys are sufficiently established, there is no reason to prolong the investigation artificially.

Move to the final trap.

# 9. The three acts

## Act 1 — First contact

The group settles into the diner while the Van cools down.

Mr. Grossi serves cold burgers and tells the story of the Mad Cook.

Then the lights flicker.

The refrigerator door opens by itself.

The Ghost of the Mad Cook bursts out screaming an impossible recipe.

### Moments to look for

- The Clumsy Duo tries to flee **while keeping their food**: a perfect moment for their standalone roll.
- The Handsome Guy immediately announces a wildly disproportionate strategy.
- The Brain notices that something about the apparition does not add up.
- The Pretty Airhead may trigger an early Accidental Charm or Oops.

The ghost disappears through a dumbwaiter or service passage before it can be caught.

Leave at least one small clue behind.

## Act 2 — Exploring the motel

The Handsome Guy now has an excellent reason to say:

> **"Let's split up."**

Do not resist the trope.

Cut quickly between the subgroups.

### Room 13

The Pretty Airhead finds a sealed room with an enormous mirror and a wobbly coat rack.

The ghost appears.

An Oops may make the wall rotate and reveal the dumbwaiter network: **the Trick Key**.

Otherwise, the same information can be discovered more directly, but with a complication.

### The boiler room

The Brain examines the building's pipes and mechanisms.

She may find:

- phosphorescent paint;
- a recently installed pulley;
- a dry-cleaning ticket in Zellani's name;
- a scrap of fabric from the costume.

Even a Defeat should give her something to reason from, even if it covers her in soot or makes her lose her glasses.

### The kitchen and storeroom

The Clumsy Duo is obviously looking for food.

A chase can send them through a movable shelf into a sealed room containing old bottles or an entrance to the cellar.

They may therefore discover **the Motive Key** completely by accident.

A large Cross-Resonance Success should produce a ridiculous but extremely effective chain of events.

### Grossi's office

If the players pursue a more rational lead, they can find Zellani's repeated purchase offers and an old plan of the motel.

This scene can provide any missing Key without forcing a unique solution.

## Act 3 — The Grand Trap

Once the three Keys have been gathered, the group knows the Ghost is human and needs to be captured.

This is the Handsome Guy's moment.

He designs a plan using as many absurd elements from the fiction as possible:

- luggage carts;
- tomato sauce;
- giant fan;
- clothesline;
- diner bell;
- freezer door;
- equipment found in the Van.

Turn these truths into Bets when they genuinely matter to the Focus.

Then roll.

### If the Handsome Guy wins

The trap works... on the wrong target.

The Duo ends up in the net.

The cage traps the Brain.

The monster escapes the device exactly as it planned.

But the movement of the scene should open the possibility of a second rebound.

### If he loses

The trap collapses.

The sauce spills.

The fan starts blowing the wrong way.

The cart slams into the refrigerator door.

And the Ghost ends up knocked out, tied up, or stuck exactly where nobody expected.

The Handsome Guy can then explain that this was obviously part of the plan.

---

# 10. Epilogue

The group pulls off the mask.

> **"Mr. Zellani?!"**

Zellani explains his plan in far too much detail.

Then comes the mandatory line:

> "And I would have gotten away with it if it weren't for you and that blasted dog!"

Mr. Grossi thanks the group.

The Van mysteriously starts again without anyone really knowing why.

Someone suggests stopping for food at the next gas station.

The GM already knows it will probably be haunted.

# 11. Recommended timeline

## Character and Van creation — 15 to 20 minutes

Reading the archetypes should be immediate.

Let the players:

- keep or cross out keywords;
- invent one or two;
- quickly define their relationships;
- create the Van together.

This phase also works as an icebreaker.

## Rules explanation — about 5 minutes

Explain only:

- fiction comes first;
- what matters becomes a Bet;
- a Bet normally gives one D6;
- evens are Successes;
- every archetype has a particular twist;
- the GM adjudicates relevance quickly;
- nobody is going to die in a chase with a plastic ghost.

The rest can be discovered through play.

## Act 1 — 20 to 30 minutes

Arrival, exposition, burger, first appearance, first chase.

## Act 2 — 45 to 60 minutes

The heart of the investigation.

Switch quickly between groups and provide the three Keys as soon as the players' actions justify them.

Once all three are established, cut to the next act.

## Act 3 — 20 to 30 minutes

Impossible plan, rolls, reversals, capture, and reveal.

# 12. What this implementation tests

Resonance: Scooby-Doo is deliberately small.

It shows that a Resonance implementation can work with:

- a few paragraphs of lore;
- sheets made almost entirely of keywords;
- a grammar of relevance built from a trope rather than a cosmology;
- asymmetric Prisms;
- several ways of reading the same D6s;
- a Prism with a standalone result;
- minimal preparation;
- a complete game in one short evening.

The contrast with Glorantha Perspectives is deliberate.

> **Glorantha tests depth.**  
> **Scooby-Doo tests compression.**

Between the two, Resonance's design space remains open.
