# Accessibility and burden distribution

:::info[Status]
**Active research program.** Resonance currently works mainly on access to practice: cognitive burden, preparation, improvisation, the blank page, and the GM role. This page does not claim to cover accessibility as a whole.
:::

Saying that a game is “more accessible” is insufficient until we specify **for whom**, **to which activity**, and **where the burden went**.

A game can remove rules while demanding more improvisation. It can remove the GM while distributing former GM functions across all players. It can reduce preparation while asking the facilitator to create more coherence in real time. It can offer very few mechanical options because it has strongly reduced the fictional space.

Resonance therefore treats accessibility as a problem of **distributing functions and burdens**, not as a synonym for minimalism.

## The control question

> **When something becomes easier for someone, what happened to the work that used to be necessary?**

Possible destinations include:

- **removed** — the function was unnecessary or redundant;
- **pre-structured** — the designer performed some of the work in advance;
- **externalized** — a support, card, token, or interface carries the function;
- **distributed** — several participants share a burden previously concentrated in one place;
- **localized** — complexity is processed only when it becomes relevant;
- **automated by fictional causality** — the Situation indicates what can react without arbitrary invention;
- **transferred** — somebody else simply carries the difficulty;
- **removed through fictional reduction** — the game became easier because fewer things can happen or mean something.

The last two cases are especially important to detect: they can create an impression of elegance without solving the accessibility problem.

## Three forms of access currently studied

### Procedural access

Being able to understand what the game expects and perform its gestures without disproportionate prior learning.

Typical questions: how much must be memorized? How often must rules be consulted? Are procedural decisions themselves interesting? Can the player understand what they may attempt without knowing a catalogue of abilities?

### Generative access

Being able to continue playing when one does not spontaneously know what to invent.

A rule such as “improvise an interesting scene” is short but may require a great deal of implicit expertise. Resonance seeks devices that provide handles without deciding in place of the participant.

The **player's blank page** and the work on **Situation** are the clearest experiments in this direction.

### Access to alterity

Being able to play from a position distant from one's own habits: another subjectivity, culture, cosmology, or sense of what matters.

Here accessibility must not mean assimilation. Reducing an unfamiliar cosmology to a few familiar categories may make play easier while destroying precisely the difference we wanted to make playable.

See [**Access to fictional alterity**](../otherness).

## Player and GM: a symmetry

The GM's entry cost reveals the invisible burdens of traditional RPG practice particularly well. One person may be expected to know the world, rules, and scenario, portray NPCs, improvise, preserve coherence, create pacing, and guarantee that “something interesting” happens.

Simply saying “the GM decides” or “the GM improvises” does not reduce that burden; it makes it less visible.

The same phenomenon exists for players. “You can do anything” may be immense freedom for an experienced player and a blank page for another.

Resonance therefore seeks to **reduce dependence on implicit expertise without reducing fictional freedom**.

## A provisional topology of burden

To analyze a game, we can ask where the following primarily reside:

- memory of the world;
- memory of the rules;
- generation of situations;
- determination of relevance;
- management of uncertainty;
- causal coherence;
- authority to decide;
- inspiration;
- pacing;
- cultural or genre knowledge.

These functions can reside in the GM, a player, the group, the text, a procedure, a material support, a digital tool, or the fiction already established.

This map is more useful than a raw rule count: it shows **who or what actually carries the game**.

## Accessibility is not easiness

An accessible experience may remain demanding, disturbing, tragic, or intellectually complex.

Unmind, for example, has no reason to become emotionally “easy”. The research question is rather whether players can generate complex psychological relations without first mastering a complicated psychological model.

> **Making a practice accessible does not mean making its experience harmless.**

## Current limits

Resonance should not use the word accessibility as an undemonstrated general claim. Sensory, motor, neurocognitive, linguistic, economic, and social accessibility each require real work, affected participants, and specific testing.

The laboratory can welcome those questions, but its current corpus mainly supports research on:

- entry cost;
- cognitive and procedural burden;
- inspiration and improvisation;
- GM burden;
- fictional distance.

## Questions to test

- Does reducing rules actually reduce burden for a first-time player?
- Which devices reduce the gap between experienced and new players without constraining the former?
- How can burden transferred to the GM or to social negotiation be identified?
- Can a procedure teach its own gesture through practice rather than prior explanation?
- How far can a game support a player without inspiration without confiscating their decisions?
- Which forms of support provide access to unfamiliar fiction without normalizing it?

Resonance's accessibility question is therefore never only “is it simpler?”

> **It is: who can now play, what can they access, and what had to be moved or preserved for that to become possible?**
