# Generative simplicity, complication, and complexity

:::info[Status]
**Structuring working hypothesis.** This distinction now underpins the research program, but its practical consequences still need to be tested across more games and audiences.
:::

Resonance does not seek to oppose a **simple game** to **complex fiction**.

That opposition rests on a confusion. In many fields, simple local rules or transformations can produce complex global behavior. Cellular automata, dynamical systems, and fractals provide obvious intellectual models for this intuition: the richness of the result is not proportional to the number of rules that generate it.

Resonance is interested in this principle of **generation** rather than exhaustive representation.

> **A game does not need to contain all the complexity it will be able to produce.**

## Three notions not to confuse

### Simplicity

A structure is simple when it relies on a small number of principles, relations, or transformations coherent enough to understand and manipulate.

Simplicity is not an absolute value. A very short rule may be difficult to practice if it transfers all interpretation or invention onto the participant.

### Complication

Complication is the **mediation burden** required to play:

- rules and exceptions to memorize;
- conversions and calculations;
- lookup;
- preparation;
- prior knowledge;
- coordination;
- improvisation without handles;
- procedural decisions that are not themselves part of the intended experience.

Complication can be useful or necessary. The problem begins when it is treated as the natural price of fictional richness.

### Complexity

Complexity means richness produced through relations and interactions:

- multiple causalities crossing one another;
- decisions with delayed consequences;
- transforming relationships;
- compatible or contradictory truths;
- irreducible perspectives;
- patterns, histories, and structures that emerge without being written in advance.

Such complexity can be **desirable**. It is often exactly what we seek in living fiction.

## Four possible combinations

A game can therefore be:

- **simple and complex** — few generators, much emergence;
- **simple and poor** — few rules, few handles, few consequences;
- **complicated and complex** — a high burden genuinely supports substantial richness;
- **complicated and poor** — much manipulation for little fictional power.

Resonance is not searching for “the fewest rules”. It asks **which complications are fertile, which are inherited, and which simple structures can carry or generate more complexity than they demand from the player**.

## Generative simplicity

We can call a device **generatively simple** when a few principles let play produce situations that do not need to be pre-written into the system.

A good generative primitive does not necessarily tell the player everything they can do. It gives them an operation that remains usable on new fictional content.

This is one way to reread Regard: the question “what matters here?” does not try to model in advance every thing that might become relevant. It tries to let relevance be **localized when it matters**.

Likewise, Situation/Vector does not try to write every possible scene. It seeks causal structures strong enough for scenes to emerge.

## A personal affinity, not a historical lineage

This sensitivity also has an affinity with a reading of the *Yijing* that matters to the personal design path behind Resonance: a very restricted formal vocabulary can open an immense space of configurations, relations, and transformations whose meaning depends on the situation.

This is not a claim that the *Yijing* is a modern theory of complex systems, nor an appeal to it as an authority for game design. The interest lies in a **kinship of attention**: few primitives, many possible transformations, and meaning that cannot be separated from the situation in which it is read.

## A methodological consequence

Whenever a design becomes lighter, the right question is not only:

> “How many rules did we remove?”

but:

> **“What generative power did we preserve or gain?”**

And conversely, whenever a rule or procedure adds burden:

> **“What new complexity does it make accessible or able to emerge?”**

This double question prevents Resonance from confusing minimalism with accessibility.

## Hypotheses to test

- Can a shorter procedure increase actual burden by requiring more implicit expertise?
- Can devices be compared by the relation between practice burden and generative power without reducing that power to a naive metric?
- Which forms of complication are useful because they externalize a difficulty the player would otherwise have to carry mentally?
- At what point does a generative constraint stop opening fiction and start closing it?
- Can games be designed where complexity appears primarily in relations produced during play rather than in rules or prior lore?

The aim is not simply to make simple games.

> **The aim is to discover forms simple enough to give access to a complexity that can exceed them.**
