# Comparisons

Resonance does not exist in a vacuum. Many of the laboratory's questions have already been explored—sometimes brilliantly—by other games.

This page is therefore not a list of “influences,” nor an attempt to claim that Resonance is unprecedented. It uses other games as **comparative cases**:

1. what related question did they encounter?
2. what concrete design solution did they choose?
3. what does that solution make possible?
4. what is Resonance still trying to understand beyond that case?

Similarity does not imply lineage. Lineage does not imply that two projects pursue the same goal.

## The foundational lineage — from wargame to role-playing game

Resonance's first lineage is not a particular rules system. It is the **role-playing game as an activity**.

Historical documents studied by Jon Peterson trace a path through **Braunstein**, Dave Arneson's experiments around **Blackmoor**, and the publication of *Dungeons & Dragons* in 1974. The exact forms changed, but something new gradually emerged in and around wargaming: participants inhabited individual characters in an adjudicated world, their decisions could exceed a closed list of actions, and consequences could persist from one session to the next.

For Resonance, this history raises a question different from mechanical genealogy:

> **What in that historical shift still belongs to the core activity of role-playing—and what was merely a contingent 1974 solution that later became convention?**

Dice, levels, classes, a single GM, synchronous speech, campaigns, or detailed character sheets should therefore not automatically be confused with the activity itself.

**What Resonance inherits:** the possibility of playing from a situated position in a world and discovering the consequences of actions that are not fully pre-written.

**What Resonance investigates:** how far can the device change—solo play, writing, silence, other distributions of authority—while preserving or transforming that activity?

Sources: [The Making of Original D&D: 1970–1977](https://wpn.wizards.com/en/products/the-making-of-original-d-and-d-1970-1977), [Jon Peterson — History of D&D in 12 Treasures](https://playingattheworld.blogspot.com/2014/01/history-of-d-in-12-treasures.html).

## HeroQuest / QuestWorlds — describing characters with words that remain playable

### Question encountered

> **How can a character's fictional richness remain playable without being translated entirely into a closed taxonomy of skills?**

*HeroQuest*, and later *QuestWorlds*, lets characters be described extensively through natural-language **abilities** and **keywords** that gather related capabilities.

A word or short phrase can therefore preserve much of its fictional meaning while still carrying a rating usable in a contest. The QuestWorlds SRD explicitly presents keywords as “umbrella abilities” from which more specific breakout abilities can emerge.

### Solution

The solution is a **compact semantic interface**: a character can be described with fictionally meaningful terms instead of a universal exhaustive skill list.

This lets the same system accept very different settings and resolve conflicts at different scales without modeling every detail of the world in advance.

### What Resonance takes from it

Regard shares this trust in fictional language. A truth such as “blood debt to the clan” or “initiate of Orlanth” can be playable because it already means something in the fiction.

### Important difference

QuestWorlds nevertheless provides a relatively stable resolution engine to which abilities connect.

Resonance pushes the question further:

> **Can fiction determine not only which ability applies, but what enters resolution, how relevance is structured, and sometimes how uncertainty itself works?**

The kinship is strong at the semantic level, while Resonance's ambition is to let the world or intended experience **change more than the inputs to the engine**.

Source: [QuestWorlds System Reference Document](https://www.chaosium.com/content/FreePDFs/QuestWorlds/QuestWorlds%20SRD.pdf).

## Fate — fictional statements as permissions and mechanical levers

### Question encountered

> **How can a fictionally meaningful statement genuinely matter to the system?**

Fate answers with **Aspects**. An Aspect can describe a character, relationship, situation, place, or consequence. The SRD emphasizes a crucial point: Aspects are **always true**. They therefore do more than grant bonuses; they can grant or withdraw fictional permission.

When invoked, an Aspect can also modify a roll. When compelled, it creates a complication and feeds the Fate Point economy.

### Solution

Fate elegantly connects three layers:

- a fictional statement;
- its consequences for what is possible in the world;
- a mechanical economy of invocations and complications.

The system thus rewards players for allowing defining traits to make their characters' lives more complicated.

### What Resonance takes from it

Fate is a major precedent for the idea that natural-language statements can become mechanical objects without losing their fictional meaning.

### Important difference

In Fate, most Aspects share a **common mechanical grammar**: invocation against a resource, +2 or reroll, compel, creation of advantages.

Regard asks a different question: a truth becomes a Bet because it is **relevant here**, not because it belongs to a pre-existing category that can be activated with a Fate Point. And at the higher Resonance level, there is no assumption that every game should translate its truths into the same economy.

> **Fate shows that a sentence can remain true and become mechanical. Resonance asks how far that sentence—and the world it belongs to—can help determine the mechanic itself.**

Source: [Fate Condensed — Aspects and Fate Points](https://fate-srd.com/fate-condensed/aspects-and-fate-points).

## Apocalypse World / PbtA — designing procedures from what the game should produce

### Question encountered

> **How can rules push the table toward the activity, tensions, and genre the game actually wants to explore?**

*Apocalypse World* does not merely place a generic resolution system under a post-apocalyptic setting. Its **moves**, playbooks, MC principles, threats, and economies structure the kinds of actions, complications, and relationships the game wants to bring into play.

The MC receives an explicit agenda and principles—including “play to find out what happens”—which make the **way the game is facilitated** part of the system.

### Solution

PbtA's major contribution is an extraordinarily fertile design approach: begin with what characters do in this kind of fiction, write procedures that engage when that fiction occurs, and structure the MC's role so it produces the intended pressures.

A crucial point for comparison is that Vincent Baker explicitly describes PbtA as an **approach to game system design**, not a single engine. He also stresses that 2d6+stat, playbooks, XP, and other conventions are not mandatory invariants. A prototype may even begin as PbtA and later abandon all formal resemblance while still having benefited from the method.

### Why this is a serious challenge for Resonance

It would be too easy to define Resonance as “starting from the experience rather than a generic engine”: PbtA already explores that idea powerfully.

The distinction Resonance must make more precise lies elsewhere, especially in:

- **attention** as an explicit object of design;
- **situated relevance** as material for resolution;
- the possibility that a world or perspective transforms not only available moves but the structure of uncertainty itself;
- the desired continuity between experience, procedure, materials, information flow, and transformation;
- the refusal to assume even one recognizable family morphology across the games produced.

That distinction is a **hypothesis to demonstrate**, not an established advantage.

> **PbtA is probably one of the strongest counter-tests of Resonance's specificity: if Resonance cannot clearly explain what additional research question it brings, it risks being a less mature restatement of an existing design approach.**

Sources: [Vincent Baker — Powered by the Apocalypse, part 1](https://lumpley.games/2019/12/30/powered-by-the-apocalypse-part-1/), [What is PbtA?](https://lumpley.games/2023/11/22/what-is-pbta/).

## Polaris — turning conversation itself into mechanism

### Question encountered

> **Must mechanics pass through numbers, dice, or cards, or can they be embedded directly in the form of conversation?**

In Ben Lehman's *Polaris: Chivalric Tragedy at Utmost North*, authority normally concentrated in a GM is distributed among several roles relative to the protagonist of a scene, including the **Heart**, the **Mistaken**, and the **Moons**.

More importantly, conflicts are structured through **ritual phrases**. Specific formulations allow participants to accept a proposal under a condition, escalate it, reject it, demand a concession, or bring the negotiation toward closure.

### Solution

Polaris turns the **syntax of negotiation** into a resolution system. Tragedy is not merely prescribed by the text: it is supported by the ways players are permitted to answer one another.

The form of dialogue becomes a game-design technology.

### What Resonance takes from it

Polaris shows very clearly that system can reside in **conversational gestures themselves**, and that the distribution of authority can be designed just as deliberately as a probability table.

It is an important precedent for research into devices, the GM role, and the alignment of play form with intended experience.

### Important difference

Polaris is extraordinarily specific: its ritual form is designed for its particular chivalric tragedy.

Resonance does not seek to generalize ritual phrases. It is interested in the design move that reaches the conclusion:

> **“For this exact experience, this form of conversation should become the mechanic.”**

Sources: [RPGnet — Polaris](https://www.rpg.net/reviews/archive/13/13254.phtml), [Polaris Notes — roles and conflict structure](https://www.darkshire.net/jhkim/rpg/polaris/).

## Dread — physically feeling accumulated risk

### Question encountered

> **How can a game produce fear and tension without asking players to simulate those feelings only through roleplay?**

*Dread* replaces dice and numbers with a Jenga-style block tower. When an action is dangerous, a player pulls from the tower. Pull and succeed; refuse and fail. As play continues, the shared tower usually becomes more fragile. If it falls, the consequences become catastrophic for the character according to the story.

### Solution

The device does several things at once:

- difficulty becomes **physically perceptible**;
- each success often raises future tension;
- risk becomes collective because everyone shares the same tower;
- the player physically experiences hesitation analogous to what the fiction wants to evoke;
- refusing to pull turns risk into a conscious choice rather than a passive random result.

### What Resonance takes from it

Dread is an almost canonical case of **resonance between device and experience**. The tower is not merely an unusual die used for any genre: its material behavior matters because it produces anticipation, fragility, and a catastrophic threshold.

### Open question

Resonance asks how to generalize not the tower but the **reasoning that led to the tower**:

> which property of the intended experience should become perceptible, and what device makes it operational?

Source: [The Impossible Dream — Dread](https://www.tiltingatwindmills.net/games/).

## Ten Candles — making time, light, and destruction part of game structure

### Question encountered

> **How can an announced tragedy remain meaningful when survival is impossible?**

*Ten Candles* declares from the beginning that it is **tragic horror**: the characters will not survive.

Ten candles are lit. They provide the actual light of play, create atmosphere, and act as a countdown. The game also allows players to physically burn parts of their character material.

### Solution

The remaining light becomes simultaneously:

- atmosphere;
- information about the state of play;
- pacing;
- a materialization of diminishing hope;
- an irreversible structure for the session.

The question is therefore no longer “can we win and survive?” but what characters do with the time they have left and what remains meaningful when final victory is unavailable.

### What Resonance takes from it

Ten Candles shows that **the arc of an entire session** can be embodied by the physical device, not merely a local resolution procedure.

It matters particularly to Resonance because it forces analysis beyond the test mechanic: duration, light, destruction, foreknowledge of the ending, and table ritual together form a system.

Source: [Cavalry Games — Ten Candles](https://cavalrygames.com/ten-candles-info).

## Alice is Missing — removing spoken conversation to change the nature of play

### Question encountered

> **What happens to role-playing when spoken conversation around the table disappears?**

*Alice is Missing* is explicitly presented as a **silent** role-playing game. After preparation, most of the game takes place over 90 minutes through **text messages** between characters. There is no formal GM, although one participant facilitates the rules.

### Solution

The communication channel is not merely a convenient way to play remotely. It directly transforms the experience:

- each character has a textual presence;
- conversations can cross, diverge, and arrive with delays;
- the table's physical silence intensifies the absence at the center of the game;
- the phone, normally external or disruptive to table play, becomes the primary fictional space;
- removing a formal GM shifts responsibility for progression toward the game's device and materials.

### What Resonance takes from it

Alice is Missing is a major case for the field of **RPG medium and boundaries**. It shows that a convention often assumed to be constitutive—speaking together aloud—can be removed and replaced with another channel that produces a distinctly role-playing experience.

The Resonance question becomes less “can an RPG be adapted to writing?” and more:

> **what forms of play become possible only when writing is designed as the primary medium?**

Source: [Renegade Game Studios — Alice is Missing](https://renegadegamestudios.com/alice-is-missing/).

## Bluebeard's Bride — distributing one identity among several players

### Question encountered

> **Must one character correspond to one player, and must a perspective be a stable unit?**

In *Bluebeard's Bride*, players do not each portray a different character. They play different psychic facets of **one Bride**—including Fear, Rage, Shame, and Desire—as she explores Bluebeard's mansion.

The game is PbtA, but its architecture uses that foundation to make internal division central. Rolls produce choices around suffering, revelation, and interpretation of the horrors encountered.

### Solution

The game turns a psychological question into a concrete distribution of authority:

- several players speak from one fictional identity;
- their positions can conflict without becoming separate characters;
- control of the character and interpretation of what happens can circulate;
- investigation of the house is simultaneously an investigation of the Bride and of the structures of violence the game explores.

### What Resonance takes from it

Bluebeard's Bride is especially useful for research on **perspective** and the **boundaries of embodiment**. It reminds us that “one player = one character = one interiority” is a device convention, not a requirement of the medium.

It is also an important example of how radically a PbtA game can transform the usual units of character and point of view.

Source: [Magpie Games — Play Bluebeard's Bride](https://magpiegames.com/pages/play-bluebeards-bride).

## What these comparisons teach Resonance

These games make vague claims difficult to sustain, such as:

- “rules should serve the experience”;
- “fiction should matter”;
- “materials can be thematic”;
- “system can produce genre”;
- “you can play without a traditional GM or dice.”

All of these already have a rich history.

The useful question is therefore more demanding:

> **Does Resonance have a sufficiently precise research object to explain something these successes do not already say separately?**

A provisional formulation is that Resonance studies **the generative design move that connects an intended experience to what must become relevant, and from there to the complete form of the device that makes that relevance operational**.

This formulation must remain contestable. Comparisons are not here to prove Resonance: they are here to prevent it from becoming so general that it is always true.

## Corpus to extend

This page is a starting point, not a canon. Other cases will become useful as the research fields sharpen, including:

- solo and journaling games;
- GMless and distributed-authority games;
- epistolary and asynchronous games;
- games centered on disability and actual accessibility of play;
- games whose material devices strongly transform experience;
- play and design traditions outside the dominant Anglophone corpus.

The goal is not to accumulate references, but to build a **comparative corpus that puts the laboratory's hypotheses under pressure**.
