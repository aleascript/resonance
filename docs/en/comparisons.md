# Comparative cases

Resonance uses other games as **comparative cases**, not as foils or a simple influence list.

Each subpage asks four questions:

1. **what nearby problem does the game encounter?**
2. **what concrete solution does it choose?**
3. **what does that solution make possible, and what burden does it move?**
4. **what does the case teach or leave open for Resonance?**

Similarity does not imply lineage, and lineage does not imply the same aim.

## Chronology

Cases are ordered by the **first publication date of the game or lineage represented**. When a page spans several editions, its main milestones are shown.

- **1969–1974** — [**Braunstein → Blackmoor → D&D 1974**](./comparisons/origins.md) — David Wesely; Dave Arneson; Gary Gygax & Dave Arneson — the emergence of role-playing as an activity and the distinction between a founding gesture and historical conventions.
- **2000–2020** — [**HeroQuest / QuestWorlds**](./comparisons/questworlds.md) — Robin D. Laws and subsequent developers — natural language as a compact semantic interface.
- **2003 / 2013** — [**Fate / Fate Core**](./comparisons/fate.md) — Rob Donoghue & Fred Hicks; the Fate Core team — true fictional statements, permissions, and mechanical economy.
- **2005** — [**Dread**](./comparisons/dread.md) — Epidiah Ravachol & Nat “woodelf” Barmore — risk made physically perceptible through the tower.
- **2005** — [**Polaris**](./comparisons/polaris.md) — Ben Lehman — conversation syntax and distributed authority as mechanics.
- **2009 / 2011** — [**Ribbon Drive**](./comparisons/ribbon-drive.md) — Avery Alder — playlists and music as a generative device.
- **2010** — [**Apocalypse World / PbtA**](./comparisons/pbta.md) — D. Vincent Baker; Meguey Baker joined as co-designer for 2e — procedures designed from fictional activity and a major counter-test for Resonance's specificity.
- **2013** — [**The Quiet Year**](./comparisons/the-quiet-year.md) — Avery Alder — map, deck, and temporality as generators of community complexity.
- **2014 / 2018** — [**Dream Askew**](./comparisons/dream-askew.md) — Avery Alder — distributed authority, setting elements, and an economy of vulnerability.
- **2015** — [**Ten Candles**](./comparisons/ten-candles.md) — Stephen Dewey — light, time, and destruction as irreversible structure.
- **2017** — [**Bluebeard's Bride**](./comparisons/bluebeards-bride.md) — Whitney “Strix” Beltrán, Marissa Kelly & Sarah Richardson — one fictional identity distributed across several players.
- **2017** — [**Monsterhearts 2**](./comparisons/monsterhearts-2.md) — Avery Alder — mechanical constraint and relational density.
- **2020** — [**Alice is Missing**](./comparisons/alice-is-missing.md) — Spenser Starke — silence, phones, and text as the primary medium.

These games matter precisely because they prevent Resonance from claiming overly broad ideas such as “rules should serve experience” or “a light device can produce rich fiction” as novelty. The laboratory's work must remain more precise: **understand how burden is distributed and how a practicable form provides access to complexity that can exceed it.**
